Cyber Kill Chain: seven stages of an attack

Pick where your defense sits, then press Play. The attacker advances one stage at a time until a control blocks them. Stopping early costs the attacker the most.

☠

Pros

  • Simple, memorable model for planning layered defenses.
  • Shows the earlier you break the chain, the cheaper the incident.

Cons

  • Assumes a linear attack; real intrusions loop and skip steps.
  • Perimeter-focused; weaker for insiders and cloud abuse.

Where it works

Defense: originated at Lockheed Martin from military “kill chain” thinking; used in government and defense contractor security operations. Commercial: banks and retailers map alerts to stages in their SOC (Security Operations Center). Other: training and tabletop exercises in hospitals and utilities.

MITRE ATT&CK: 14 tactics, many techniques

ATT&CK describes the why (tactic) and how (technique and sub-technique) of adversary behavior. Click a tactic, or run a campaign to see the order attackers typically work in. Example from the matrix: T1547, Boot or Logon Autostart Execution, sits under Persistence.

Pros

  • Detailed, shared vocabulary built from observed attacks.
  • Each technique lists detections and mitigations.

Cons

  • Large; teams need prioritizing to avoid coverage theater.
  • Describes behavior, not how likely you are to be hit.

Where it works

Commercial: security vendors tag detections with ATT&CK IDs, and red teams plan tests with it. Government and defense: widely adopted by public agencies for threat modeling and sharing. Other: health and energy sectors use it to compare defenses to known threat groups.

Risk basics: threat, vulnerability, impact, likelihood

Likelihood and impact lab

Expected yearly loss = likelihood × impact × (1 − control strength)

“Nines” reliability

A process that is 99% reliable fails once per 100 runs. More nines cost more effort.

Key terms

Threat: event that could harm assets. Vulnerability: the “hole in the fence”. Impact: size of harm. Likelihood: annualized probability. Threat agents: intentional (insiders, APTs, fraudsters), unintentional (errors, misconfigurations), and hazards (storms, power failure).

A register is one central place for every known risk. Partitioning it by business unit helps focus but can hide global risks, so keep a roll-up view. Protect it: it is sensitive.

Pros

  • Turns security into business terms leaders can fund.
  • Central view replaces scattered data sources.

Cons

  • Likelihood is subjective, weak for new technology.
  • Existential risks resist simple modeling.

Where it works

Commercial: insurers and underwriters model loss events daily; boards review registers. Defense and government: formal risk assessment reports (RAR) before systems go live. Other: hospitals and airlines rank critical processes such as emergency triage or flight checks.

PASTA and attack trees

PASTA (Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis) moves through stages: define objectives, identify threats, analyze vulnerabilities, simulate attacks. Attack trees put the attacker’s goal at the root and split it into sub-goals:

Steal customer data
├─ Phish an employee ── harvest password ── log in
├─ Exploit public web app ── SQL injection
└─ Insider copy ── USB or cloud upload

Pros

  • Finds design flaws before attackers do.
  • Covers privacy, safety and non-repudiation, not only CIA.

Cons

  • Needs skilled people and time.
  • DREAD scores are subjective and can vary by rater.

Where it works

Commercial: software firms run STRIDE in design reviews; STRIDE was popularized at Microsoft. Defense: attack trees and simulation for weapons and communications systems. Other: medical device and automotive makers model safety threats.

Risk visibility: IOCs, sharing and CVSS

Indicators of compromise: classify the evidence

Click an item.

IOC is evidence a breach happened or is happening; types include URLs, domain names, IP addresses and hash values. IOA (indicator of attack) is an IOC signaled live by ongoing events. Precursors hint at future attacks and let you raise a “risk throttle”, for example before a charged political event or after bad press.

Sharing threat intelligence

Organizations share IOCs, analyst notes and remediation steps internally and through ISACs and ISAOs, which give sector-specific threat data. Some firms hesitate because disclosure may expose issues; the payoff is faster remediation when similar attacks appear.

CVSS severity

0 None, 0.1 to 3.9 Low, 4.0 to 6.9 Medium, 7.0 to 8.9 High, 9.0 to 10.0 Critical. Created 2005, maintained by FIRST.org, version 4 released 2023.

Pros

  • Shared intelligence speeds response.
  • CVSS gives a common patch-priority scale.

Cons

  • CVSS ignores your own asset value and context.
  • Some IOCs need heavy analysis, so they are not real-time.

Where it works

Commercial: financial firms exchange threat data through their sector ISAC; scanners rank findings by CVSS. Government and defense: national vulnerability databases publish CVSS scores. Other: health and energy ISACs share indicators among hospitals and utilities.
